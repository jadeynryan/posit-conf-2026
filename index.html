⏱ 2:00 · Jadey

Jadey: This story began about 3 years ago when I gave my very first presentation at posit::conf in 2023.

During that talk, I showed the audience how we used a parameterized Quarto template to magically render hundreds of soil health reports for each farmer who participated in our project in Washington State.

Each of the 350+ farmers got 2 versions of the report: one interactive HTML file that lets them click around their plots and one printable PDF for those who wanted paper. These reports included background information on all the soil health measurements, plus plots and tables designed so a farmer could see how their fields compared against similar crops and regions. The reports were meant to be a decision-support tool, not a data dump.

One farmer said, “I was able to see a direct connection between my soil management decisions and the health of the soil I manage.”

So what was the problem?

⏱ 0:30 · Jadey

Jadey: Well, that recipe lived in exactly one kitchen. The template solved the reports problem for our specific project in Washington State. But, there was a growing need to give people outside of our project access to this workflow. We had a University in California, an adviser in Idaho, and conservation district staff from Oregon who all wanted to run reports for their own data for their own farmers.

At the time, the template was too bespoke to our project in WA and not easy for these users to adapt for their own projects.

So how did we take this template and make it easy for them to use?

⏱ 1:00 · Jadey

Jadey: Developing an R package to support these teams felt like a natural progression. For people with the right technical expertise, we wanted to give them flexibility to adapt the template to their projects and build their own soil health reports.

We broke up our one big report template into smaller, modular Quarto files and replaced all the Washington-specific components with placeholders for users to swap in information applicable to their own project.

Then we pulled a lot of the R code out of the original Quarto template and turned that code into reusable functions that handle the core work like validating, processing, and visualizing the data. We also created an R script that renders all the reports at once.

The last piece was documentation. Once the Quarto files were modularized and the code became reusable, we wanted to make sure people could understand what was available and how to use this reporting workflow. We used {roxygen2} to document all functions and {pkgdown} to create a website with tutorials.

Everything you need to adapt the workflow to your own data.

Tanya: This is where I come in. In 2024, Jadey reached out to me for help on the next evolution of the project - building a Shiny app to deliver Quarto reports. We previously connected years before via the Twitterverse and R-Ladies community- I was ecstatic to get a chance to work with her.

⏱ 0:45 · Jadey

Jadey: In September 2025, we released Dirt Data Reports into the universe, but our story doesn’t stop there. We took inventory on what worked and what didn’t and created a feedback loop with users.

We started getting some really useful signals.

On the positive side - it was clear that organizations were using the app and saving real time and effort by automating their reports.

But there was also a negative signal - once people started using it for different projects, they started pushing beyond the original assumptions and scope we had designed for. They needed more customization in some areas and more guardrails in others. This meant baking in more features to match their needs.

The app worked. It also showed us where the package was thin.

⏱ 0:15 · Jadey Jadey (land on the line): The app worked. It also showed us exactly where the package was thin. [→ Lessons Learned]

Lessons Learned

Expectation vs. reality, and the feedback loop between them.

Three layers. We assumed they'd line up.

Expectation. A neat, linear build: template, then package, then app.

Reality. Real-world use exposed workarounds in the app, signaling what the lower layers needed.

Refactoring for independence. Moving the workarounds into {soils} and the Quarto template.

⏱ 2:20 · Tanya 1:35 (11–13) / Jadey 0:45 (14)

ADD PUMPKIN SPICE COOKIE VARIATION AS EXAMPLE

Tanya, three layers (0:20, on arrival: three dots). Now, for a quick lesson in expectation vs. reality. When we started this project, the building blocks were obvious: Template, Package, and App. We expected a neat linear build, each feeding into the next.

But once we started patching gaps - data validation, new report customization functions - we rushed to make these fixes in the Shiny app and didn’t consider how the feedback should flow back to the supporting building blocks. A data validation function that lived in the app would be best served in the package, and modifications to template would flow back to the original recipe.

This [point to diagram] is where we started. On paper, when you draw it out, it should look something like this: 3 tidy boxes. We expected a neat, linear build. Template, package, and then app.

Tanya, expectation (0:30). [click: the pipeline draws] But, expectation often falls short of reality.

Once people started using the app in their own projects, they surfaced gaps. When these needs came up, the app became the place where we worked around what the package couldn’t do yet. This meant a handful of workarounds living inside the Shiny app instead of the package. Hardcoded lookups. A validation step the package didn’t do yet. Formatting the template should have owned.

Tanya, reality (0:45). [click: the tangle] This is what it actually looked like. Every arrow is something the app was doing that a lower layer should have owned. I was the first person to build a UI on top of {soils}, and every time the package didn’t have what I needed, I patched around it in the app.

Those workarounds weren’t failures. They were loud signals about what the package was missing. And you can’t see them from inside the package.

Jadey, refactoring (0:45). [click: retract and redraw; the rings top right light up fully] That was the real lesson for me as the maintainer: I didn’t know what abstractions the package needed until someone built a UI on top of it. Building layer three showed us where layers one and two needed to grow. So now we’re taking those lessons back into the package and template.

Jadey: All this comes back to knowing you who’re building for. There’s no one layer to rule them all. Most of us start at the first: we build something to make our own work easier.

Tanya: Some of us move to the second when others on our team want to be able to use and adapt it.

Jadey: And some of us go all the way to the third layer because the people who need the answer are never going to open an IDE.

Tanya: Each layer is the right layer for someone. What determines which one you need isn’t the tool or the team. It’s one question to ask before you build: who is this for?
