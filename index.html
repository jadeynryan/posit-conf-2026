Jadey: Some of you might remember me from the very last session of posit::conf 2023. Quick version: I went from scooping water samples out of streams to building 86 custom farmer reports in one week, and that skills test got me a data science job at WSDA.

By the time I gave that talk, our soil health program had sampled hundreds of fields across Washington, and every one of the farmers in it, more than 300 since 2020, got their own report. Interactive HTML with maps and plots. A printable PDF for the folks who wanted paper. And plots designed so a farmer could see their fields against similar crops and the rest of the state. Not a data dump, a decision support tool.

One farmer said, “I was able to see a direct connection between my soil management decisions and the health of the soil I manage.”

The mechanism was parameterized Quarto: treat the report as a function, shape your data so it’s subsettable, make it interpretable, make it accessible. The 2023 slides are still up if you want the code.

Jadey: We pulled everything that wasn’t Washington-specific out of the template and into an R package: {soils}. The validation, the calculations, the styling, the Quarto pieces every report shares. So the cookbook wasn’t for my team. It was for a conservation district in Oregon, a university lab, an extension office that had its own samples and its own farmers. Anyone comfortable in R could create a {soils} project, drop in their data, edit the content, and generate reports for every participant in their survey.

Lessons Learned

Expectation vs. reality, and the feedback loop between them.

Three layers. We assumed they'd line up.

Expectation. A neat, linear build: template, then package, then app.

Reality. Real-world use exposed workarounds in the app, signaling what the lower layers needed.

Refactoring for independence. The hacks move down into {soils}; every tier stands on its own.

⏱ 2:20 · Tanya 1:35 (11–13) / Jadey 0:45 (14)

Tanya, three layers (0:20, on arrival: three dots). So here’s the part we didn’t plan. When you draw this on a whiteboard it’s three tidy boxes.

Tanya, expectation (0:30). [click: the pipeline draws] The expectation was a neat, linear build. Template, then package, then app. Each layer finished before the next one starts.

But once people started using the app in their own projects, they surfaced gaps. When these needs came up, the app became the place where we worked around what the package couldn’t do yet. This meant a handful of workarounds living inside the Shiny app instead of the package. Hardcoded lookups. A validation step the package didn’t do yet. Formatting the template should have owned.

Tanya, reality (0:45). [click: the tangle] This is what it actually looked like. Every arrow is something the app was doing that a lower layer should have owned. I was the first person to build a UI on top of {soils}, and every time the package didn’t have what I needed, I patched around it in the app.

Those workarounds weren’t failures. They were loud signals about what the package was missing. And you can’t see them from inside the package.

Jadey, refactoring (0:45). [click: retract and redraw; the rings top right light up fully] That was the real lesson for me as the maintainer: you do not know what abstractions your package needs until someone tries to build a UI on top of it. Building layer three forced us to rethink layers one and two.

So we went back to the workbench. We pulled the hacks out of Tanya’s app and engineered them into {soils} as proper functions, with tests, with docs. And the whole stack got better. Now every layer stands on its own. An analyst can use the template, a coder can use the package, a farm advisor can click through the app, all on the same clean foundation. The feedback loop runs backwards through the stack, and that’s fine. That’s the point.

⏱ 0:45 · Tanya

Tanya: There’s no one layer to rule them all. Most of us start at the first: we build something to make our own lives easier. Some of us go to the second, because someone on the team asked for it. Some of us go all the way to the third, because the person who needs the answer is never going to open an IDE.

Each of those is the right layer for someone. What decides which one you need isn’t the tool, or the team, or how far you can take the code. It’s one question, asked before you build: who is this for?
